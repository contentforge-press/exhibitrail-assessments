Exhibitrail · what seven reviews show

Seven reviews, one pattern: the gap is in the evidence, not the code.

I read seven public AI codebases and one hundred and one trust centres before writing a single sales line. The code is generally better than its reputation. What is missing is the thing a buyer needs in order to decide — a statement that survives being checked.

1 · The scanner number is not the finding

Two of the reviews put 174 and 310 scanner items on the table. The issues that survived reading were one and zero. A true-positive rate near one percent is not a flaw in the tools; it is what tools do when they cannot see intent.

If a review reports “12 High” on its first page, the review has not happened yet. The number describes the tool, not the system.

This is why every candidate in these reviews starts as Untriaged, and why a withdrawn item stays in the file with its reason. A practice that only publishes what it kept cannot be checked — and cannot be wrong, which is a different thing from being right.

2 · Where the real items cluster

Not memory safety. Not injection strings. The items that survived were about boundary and authority:

Content the agent reads arriving in the same string as the instruction it should follow — no separator, no provenance, no type. A single tool holding a broad credential, protected by a sentence in the prompt. A tenant key the end user can type. A gate that holds on one path and not on the second.

These are not exotic. They are the ordinary shape of a system built quickly, and they are exactly what a security questionnaire is trying to ask about — except the questionnaire asks “do you protect against prompt injection?” and the honest answer is “we have a sentence in the prompt”.

3 · Trust centres answer the easy half

Out of 106 domains, 101 had a readable trust page. Eight themes appear across them:

ThemeShare of readable trust pages
SOC 2 / ISO 27001 mentioned76%
Retention policy stated69%
Training on customer data stated54%
Penetration testing mentioned49%
Sub-processors listed44%
Model providers named25%
Prompt injection addressed5%
Agent tool authority addressed7%

The pattern is not dishonesty; it is that compliance frameworks have names, and agent behaviour does not. A vendor can answer “are you SOC 2?” in one sentence. Nobody has taught them to answer “what can your agent change without a human approving it?” — so the page is silent, and the buyer reads silence as safety.

The two questions at 5% and 7% are the two an agent product cannot be sold into a serious enterprise without. That is the whole commercial argument for this practice, and it is a census, not an opinion.

4 · Samples are the cheapest place to fix it

62% webhook verification in an SDK's examples is not a vulnerability report. It is a forecast. Every one of those samples is copied into somebody's production service by somebody who had fifteen minutes. The fix costs a line of code and belongs in the sample, not in a security review three years later.

5 · What a buyer should do with this

Ask for the artefact, not the assurance. If the answer is a paragraph, you have learned the vendor has a template. If the answer is a list — of write actions, of gates, of stores with retention numbers and owners — you have something that can be checked next quarter, when the model has changed and the prompt has been edited in a console.

And when the answer is “none”, write it down and price it in. That is not a reason to walk away from a good product. It is a reason to know what you are buying.

6 · What this practice does not claim

Seven reviews are seven reviews. They are source reviews of public code: no running system was touched, no maintainer was asked, no score is comparable across them. Counts are counts. Where a review found nothing, it says so and shows the false positive it rejected — because a practice that finds something every time is not measuring, it is performing.
